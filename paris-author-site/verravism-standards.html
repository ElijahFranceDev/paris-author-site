The Five Laws of Verravism

These craft laws complement the twelve founding declarations and seven craft standards. They are a guide to deliberate narrative design, not legal rules or a claim that other fiction is inferior.

The Law of Certainty

A belief must be established before it can meaningfully fracture. Show why an assumption is reasonable, then challenge it. A surprise without a prior belief is not necessarily a fracture.

The Law of Perspective

No witness possesses automatic authority over the truth. Distinguish a character’s claim, the text’s evidence, and a reader’s inference. Give each meaningful limits.

The Law of Consequence

A fracture in certainty must carry a meaningful cost. Ask what decision, relationship, identity, or moral judgment changes once the belief shifts.

The Law of Fairness

The reader may be misdirected but must not be cheated. Plant discoverable evidence, preserve the causal story, and distinguish intentional withholding from accidental contradictions.

The Law of Residue

A fractured belief should leave a lasting impression. Resolve immediate narrative obligations while allowing a consequential insight or question to remain.

The Verravic Fracture Scale

A descriptive scale of the scope and intensity of fractured certainty, independent of horror, violence, page count, difficulty, or literary merit. Classify the dominant effect of the finished work, not its loudest scene.

Level I — Disturbance

A local belief is challenged; the larger interpretation remains intact.

Example: A familiar habit acquires a surprising explanation.

Level II — Instability

Contradictions recur and confidence in a key interpretation weakens.

Example: Two believable accounts challenge a relationship.

Level III — Fracture

A foundational belief is broken, changing a consequential choice or judgment.

Example: The motive for an earlier act changes the protagonist’s decision.

Level IV — Collapse

Several interconnected beliefs fail, requiring broad reassessment.

Example: Trust in a family and an institution breaks together.

Level V — Reconstruction

The reader must fundamentally reinterpret the narrative’s governing assumptions.

Example: The ending makes the same earlier events form a substantially different story.

Using the scale responsibly

Choose a level and write a one-sentence rationale naming the belief, scope of change, and consequence. When uncertain between two levels, use the lower one or describe the range. Reader responses may differ; this is not a content warning or quality grade.

Classification is a separate axis

The six classifications — perceptual, mnemonic, moral, structural, relational, and systemic — describe what kind of certainty is challenged. A relational work at Level II and a structural work at Level V are equally valid. Use the classification that best describes the effect; multiple may coexist.

Revision test

  1. What belief was established, and why was it reasonable?
  2. Which witness position changes?
  3. Can the reader locate relevant evidence on a reread?
  4. What meaningful consequence follows?
  5. Is the central action understandable without solving every ambiguity?
  6. What remains, and why?
